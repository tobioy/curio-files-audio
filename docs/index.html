Thursday, September 10

There is a very particular kind of embarrassment that only happens when something enormous, armed, and confident gets absolutely wrecked by something small, feathered, or squishy. History is stuffed with it, and once you start looking you cannot stop finding it.

Start in the wheat country of Western Australia, November 1932. Farmers had spent a season watching flightless, six foot tall birds trample their crops into confetti, so the government sent actual soldiers, with actual Lewis machine guns, to solve the emu problem the way governments solve most problems. The emus had other plans. They scattered into small, fast moving squads the moment the shooting started, the guns jammed in the dust, and after several days and thousands of rounds spent, the operation was quietly abandoned. Newspapers at the time did not treat it as a joke, they treated it as a genuine military failure, because it was one. The emus went back to eating wheat. Nobody gave them a medal, but they earned one.

Fly forward exactly seventy five years and swap the desert for the actual vacuum of space. In 2007 the European Space Agency strapped a payload of tardigrades, water bears the size of a grain of sand, onto a satellite and launched them into orbit with no protection at all. No suit, no capsule, just raw exposure to solar radiation and the total emptiness that kills a human in about fifteen seconds. The tardigrades shriveled themselves into a dried out survival state called a tun, sat there for ten days getting cooked by cosmic rays, then came home, rehydrated, and started laying eggs like nothing had happened. Researchers led by Ingemar Jonsson published the results and casually confirmed that the toughest animal on Earth might also be the toughest animal off it.

Rewind now a full century further back, to a manicured hunting ground outside Paris in 1807. Napoleon, fresh off a military triumph, asked his chief of staff Berthier to arrange a nice relaxing rabbit hunt for the top brass. Berthier, wanting to guarantee a good show, ordered up several hundred rabbits rather than trusting the local wild population. The problem was the supplier delivered farm raised rabbits, tame ones, thoroughly used to seeing a human and expecting breakfast. The moment the cages opened, the rabbits did not scatter into the woods, they sprinted straight at the hunting party, climbed Napoleon's legs, and swarmed his carriage in a fluffy, hungry, unstoppable tide. The man who had rearranged the map of Europe was chased off a field by bunnies looking for lunch.

Now step inside the Louvre, August 1911. A museum handyman named Vincenzo Peruggia had spent his shifts working near a certain famous portrait behind glass, and one slow Monday he simply hid in a closet overnight, lifted the painting off the wall the next morning, tucked it under his coat, and walked out the staff entrance. The Mona Lisa was gone for two years, and its absence did more for its reputation than four centuries on the wall ever had. Newspapers ran the story daily, tourists lined up to stare at the empty hooks, and when the painting turned up in Florence, its return was treated like a celebrity homecoming. Before the theft, it was one respected portrait among many. After it, it was the most famous painting on the planet, thanks entirely to a guy with a coat and a bad plan.

Four different problems, four different centuries, and the same lesson refusing to sink in. Underestimate the small, the tame, the microscopic, or the overlooked, and it will eventually make a fool out of whatever thinks it is in charge. The emus, the water bears, the rabbits, and one stolen painting all somehow agree on that.
